The five options

Same layout for each. Effort numbers are relative to the low-poly option (1×), which is the cheapest to produce.

1

Neon-noir realism

Downtown at 2 a.m. after the rain: black streets, magenta and cyan signs smeared across wet asphalt, deep shadows, light that glows and bleeds. Cinematic and moody.

In the spirit ofNeed for Speed (2015), Need for Speed Heat's night half, the street scenes of Cyberpunk 2077.

How damage reads
Up close it's great: reflections bend over a dent and sell the shape. At 30 m the glare, glow and reflections can hide a crumpled corner, so it needs help (scrape marks, sparks, a missing headlight).
How night chases read
Spectacular: red and blue strobes bounce off the wet road. The catch is that neon signs fight the police lights for attention, so we'd have to keep signs dimmer than light bars by rule.
Browser cost
HighThe street is drawn twice (once for the wet-road reflection), plus a glow pass and high-range colour. Roughly 1.5–2× the low-poly cost.
Art workload
About 2×Textured car paint, trim and tyres, a neon sign kit, a wet-road material, and lighting tuned per district.
Risks
Looks like a lot of other night racers. Mid-range phones will struggle. Dark cars vanish at night unless we add rim light. The colour-fringe effect annoys some players, so it needs an off switch.
2

Stylised low-poly dark

A grim diorama: chunky, faceted shapes, a muted palette, soft fog, and lights that glow without any texture detail. Calm and graphic, with grit coming from colour and lighting rather than surface detail.

In the spirit ofart of rally, Absolute Drift, the flat-colour dusk of Firewatch.

How damage reads
Best of the five. Each flat facet catches light on its own, so a crumple shows up as a cluster of broken facets you can see from across a junction. It also maps directly onto our damage lattice.
How night chases read
Clean. Nothing else in the frame is shiny, so the light bars are easily the brightest thing. Fog hides the distance cheaply and makes headlights pop.
Browser cost
LowNo textures, simple materials, a light glow pass. Runs on phones and leaves room for 24 cars and traffic.
Art workload
1× (baseline)Matches the procedural car generator we already planned: every car gets a 3–6k triangle mesh immediately, and no texture painting is needed.
Risks
Can look cheap or like a mobile game if lighting and silhouettes are lazy. Less "wow" in a trailer. Needs a strong colour script per district so streets don't blur together.
3

PS2 / early-HD throwback

A memory of 2004 street racing: chunky pixels, fog that swallows the city a block away, slightly wobbly geometry, colours with visible dither. Nostalgic and a bit lo-fi on purpose.

In the spirit ofNeed for Speed Underground 2, Midnight Club 3, Burnout 3: Takedown.

How damage reads
Weakest. At low resolution a dent 30 m away is a few pixels. We'd have to exaggerate deformation, lean on parts falling off, sparks and smoke, and accept that subtle damage disappears.
How night chases read
Fine. Lights become big glowing sprites and fog keeps the frame simple, so strobes stand out. Long sight lines are lost to fog, which changes how chases feel.
Browser cost
Very lowIt draws about a ninth of the pixels and uses the simplest lighting. The most phone-friendly option.
Art workload
About 1.3×Small hand-painted textures (128–256 px) on top of low-poly meshes. Getting the nostalgia exactly right takes taste more than hours.
Risks
Niche: some players read it as a joke or as unfinished. Feels at odds with careful sim-cade physics. Low resolution hurts the HUD and readability. Wobble can cause motion sickness at speed.
4

Graphic-novel halftone

A moving comic book: ink outlines, printed-dot shading, paper and ink only, and the police red and blue as the only colour on the page. Bold and instantly recognisable.

In the spirit ofXIII, Borderlands, the Sin City comics and film.

How damage reads
Good. Outlines trace every fold, so a crumpled corner turns into a scribble of ink lines that reads from a distance. Fine creases can flicker while moving, which needs tuning.
How night chases read
Very strong. Darkness becomes solid ink, lit areas become paper, and the strobes are the only colour, so you always know where the police are.
Browser cost
MediumAn extra pass to find edges, plus the dot pattern at full resolution. Fine on laptops, OK on newer phones.
Art workload
About 1.6×Simple meshes and flat colours, but custom shaders, per-object line tuning, and every effect (sparks, smoke, rain) has to be redrawn to fit the comic look.
Risks
The most opinionated choice: people love it or bounce. Can tire the eye over long sessions. Hard to show wetness, speed and surface detail. Dot patterns shimmer in motion if not handled carefully.
5

Realistic

As close to a photo of a real street as a browser sensibly allows: clear-coat paint with real reflections, soft shadows under the cars, wet patches on the asphalt, orange street-lamp light.

In the spirit ofWreckfest itself, Forza Horizon at night, BeamNG.drive.

How damage reads
Matches the Wreckfest reference up close: reflections warp over dents. But realism raises the bar: dents also need scratched paint, dirt and bent trim, or they look oddly clean. At 30 m, smooth shading softens the dent.
How night chases read
Real night is dark. To stay readable we have to cheat exposure and add light, which drifts back toward the neon-noir look anyway.
Browser cost
Very highShadow maps, physically based materials, reflections and high-range colour, on every car. Expect roughly half the frame rate of low-poly; phones will need heavy cut-downs.
Art workload
About 4–5×Detailed car models (20–80k triangles), painted texture sets per car and per district, level-of-detail versions, probably outsourced. Our procedural cars wouldn't be good enough.
Risks
In the browser you get "convincing", not photoreal, and "almost real" can look worse than stylised. The biggest asset bill. Realistic cars drift toward real brands (a trademark problem). Much of the work would be redone for the native engine.

Recommendation

Pick Stylised low-poly dark. Runner-up: Neon-noir realism.

Low-poly dark is what Checkpoint 3 already aims for, it is the cheapest to run in a browser and on phones, our procedural car meshes come out in that style for free, and its flat facets make dents read at 30 m better than anything else here. Neon-noir uses the same meshes with extra lighting passes (wet-road reflections, glow), so we can layer its best tricks onto low-poly later for fast machines without redoing any assets. On Realistic: in the browser, good materials and lighting get us to "convincing but not photoreal", which is what option 5 shows, at the highest cost of the five. True photorealism needs detailed scanned assets and lighting that only a native engine can deliver, so it belongs in the Godot-vs-Unreal engine decision at Checkpoint 3, not in this prototype.

About the live scene. Everything in it is generated in code: no images, no bought models, no real car brands. The cars are simplified stand-ins, and each style is a quick approximation built for this page, not finished art. The frame-rate counter is the honest part: compare styles on the same machine and you'll see the relative cost.

If your device asks for reduced motion, the camera stays still and the police lights are shown steadily on. Use the "Animate anyway" button to see it move.
